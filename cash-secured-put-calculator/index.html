The math

The cash-secured put math

A cash-secured put is a promise: you'll buy 100 shares at the strike if the stock is below it at expiration, and you hold the cash to honor that promise. The premium is your pay for making it. Three numbers describe the whole trade:

Collateral  = strike × 100 shares held in cash
Rent       = premium ÷ collateral, per month
Basis      = strike − premium per share (your cost if assigned)

Worked example: sell a $460 put for $6.50 with a month to expiration. You hold $46,000 as collateral and collect $650 — a rent of 1.41% for the month. If the put is assigned, you own the shares at a basis of $453.50, below where the stock traded when you sold the put.

Those three numbers are necessary — they are not sufficient. A calculator can't tell you whether the company is worth owning at $453.50, whether the strike sits at a sensible delta, or whether an earnings print lands inside the trade. That's the difference between a calculator and a grade.

Questions

Frequently asked

How do you calculate the return on a cash-secured put?

Divide the premium by the cash collateral (strike × 100) and scale it to the time held. $650 of premium on a $460 strike is $650 ÷ $46,000 = 1.41% for that expiration cycle — about 1.4% a month when the cycle runs a month.

What is a good delta for a cash-secured put?

Around 30 delta is the standard working range: roughly a 30% chance of finishing in the money, with premium that's still worth collecting. Much higher and you're effectively just buying the stock; much lower and the rent rarely clears 1% a month.

What happens if a cash-secured put is assigned?

You buy 100 shares per contract at the strike. Your real cost is the strike minus the premium you kept — on a $460 strike with $6.50 of premium, your basis is $453.50 a share. That's why the grade shows basis on every put: assignment should be a price you'd choose, not a surprise.

How much cash do you need to sell a cash-secured put?

The full strike × 100 per contract, held as collateral — $46,000 behind a single $460 put. If that number would strain the account, the strike is too big, whatever the premium looks like.

Should you sell a cash-secured put before earnings?

WheelGrade's print gate says no: earnings inside the expiration is a hard F, even on the best names. A print can gap a stock straight through your strike overnight, and the premium rarely pays for that risk.
